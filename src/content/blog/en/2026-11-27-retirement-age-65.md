South Korea is again debating whether to raise its legal retirement age from 60 to 65. According to Korean media, the ruling party was due to discuss options on November 27, while the country's labor unions are demanding legislation before the year ends. Here is what is driving the debate and where the sides disagree.

## The pension gap

Korea's mandatory retirement age is 60, but the national pension starts later. MBC reports that workers can face up to five years with little or no income between leaving their jobs and receiving a pension. About 4.46 million people will turn 60 over the next five years, and one in four employed people in Korea is already 60 or older.

Jobs after retirement are often security guard, cleaning or caregiving work with short contracts. That insecurity is the unions' main argument.

## What is on the table

Hankyung reported that the Democratic Party is pursuing a phased increase starting in 2029 and reaching 65 in 2037, with mandatory rehiring from 2028 to 2036 also discussed. This comes from a related-article summary rather than a bill text, so treat it as a proposal under discussion, not a settled plan. No bill text or parliamentary schedule had been confirmed in the sources we read.

## Worries about young workers

MBC says the number of employed people aged 15 to 29 fell by 196,000 year on year last month, the 48th straight monthly decline. Nearly half of small businesses surveyed said they would have to hire fewer young people if retirement ages rise. A study cited in the report estimates that after the 60-year rule took effect in 2016, full-time jobs for young people fell by about 6% over three years. Critics say extensions without wage adjustments squeeze new hiring most. The government has promised youth employment measures but says details depend on how and when the extension is set.

## Pressure on companies

Hankyung reported that at one financial firm, voluntary-retirement applications dropped to about a third of usual levels as the legislation gained speed, because high earners gain by waiting. Firms also use a system of rehiring retirees on lower-paid contracts; if the legal retirement age rises, they may have to keep existing pay and conditions instead.

## Where the sides stand

Unions want 65 and say they will protest if the ruling party's task force produces no real bill. Employers' official position was not included in the articles we reviewed; company views appeared only through anonymous HR executives.

## What to watch

- Start date and pace of any increase, and whether rehiring is used alongside it
- Whether wage-system reform and youth job measures come with the bill
- Whether the legislation actually passes this year

Retirement age touches pensions, youth jobs and corporate costs at once, so no single number settles the question. Details may change as a bill takes shape.
