South Korea's central bank raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter point on November 26, taking the base rate from 3.0% to 3.25%. The decision came at the Bank of Korea's last policy meeting of the year. It follows two consecutive hikes in July and August and a pause in October, so the bank has now returned to tightening.

## The decision at a glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Base rate | 3.0% to 3.25% (+0.25 percentage point) |
| Recent path | Raised in July and August, held in October |
| 2026 growth forecast | 3.9% (was 3.3% in August) |
| 2027 growth forecast | 4.1% (was 2.9% in August) |
| Inflation forecast | 2.7% this year, 2.4% next year |

One outlet reported the 2026 growth forecast as 3.8%, while two others reported 3.9%. This post uses 3.9%; check the central bank's own release for the final figure.

## Why the bank raised rates

Korean media point to two main reasons:

- **Strong growth.** A boom in semiconductor exports pushed third-quarter GDP up 1.0% from the previous quarter.
- **Inflation above target.** Both headline and core inflation ran at 2.8% last month, above the bank's 2% goal. Core inflation excludes volatile energy and food prices.

JoongAng Ilbo also noted that bonus payouts at chipmakers next year could lift consumer spending and add to demand-driven price pressure.

## What about market jitters?

Stocks at home and abroad have recently corrected, raising worries about financial stability. Reports say the bank put more weight on solid real-economy data such as chip prices, exports and the current account.

## What to watch next

- **More hikes?** A Kiwoom Securities analyst expects another 0.25-point increase in February, which would put the rate at 3.50%. That is one analyst's forecast, not a bank commitment.
- **Household debt.** Chosun Ilbo pointed out that higher rates raise interest burdens for indebted households.
- **Uneven recovery.** The upswing is concentrated in semiconductors, which leaves other sectors behind.

## Why it matters

A higher base rate generally pushes up borrowing costs on variable-rate loans and deposit rates too, though the speed and size differ by lender and product. This post is general background, not investment or financial advice.
